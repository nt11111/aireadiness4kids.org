Rules sound clear until they meet a real case. Here you write one and attack it.

<TryIt offline={"No AI tool? Write the rule yourself, swap with a partner, and each find three ways the other's rule breaks."} warning={"Don't type real names or details about real students into an AI tool."}>
Use an AI tool your school allows, or the ARK practice tool once it launches. Ask it to draft a one-sentence rule for AI use at your school, such as a rule about AI on homework.

Then red-team the rule. Ask the tool for three situations where the rule fails or treats a student unfairly. Push it: "What about a student who uses AI to translate?" "What if a teacher's AI detector is wrong?"
</TryIt>

Now fix your rule with the Collingridge dilemma in mind. Answer four questions in writing:

1. Who enforces it?
2. What happens to a student who is wrongly accused, and who do they appeal to?
3. When will the rule be reviewed? Add a date.
4. Who has the authority to adopt it? Name the person or body, such as your principal, a school site council, or the school board. "The school" is not an answer.

Check what the AI gave you, too. It may have invented a policy or a law. You learned why in Module 1.

Rules above your school have a door too. Anyone can submit a public comment on a proposed US federal rule. It is not a vote. Agencies must respond to relevant, significant comments, and reasoned comments with evidence carry the most weight.
