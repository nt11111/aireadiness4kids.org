**Course:** AI Fluent (9-12). **Writer:** Neil. **Peer checker:** Viren.

**Status:** Full draft, revised October 14, 2026 with 2026 data and ARK's view. Written with Claude from Neil's direction. Claude opened every source below. Neil still needs to open each link and read the module aloud before peer check.

**On the site:** Students examine how AI is changing job markets and what skills will matter in an AI-saturated economy.[ Slide deck](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1zfsrItHexjCnuXBPWfEfaLmq7IwV6hnd/view)

**Key topics:**

- Jobs AI is automating vs. jobs it's augmenting
- Skills that remain distinctly human: creativity, judgment, empathy
- AI and economic inequality: who benefits, who is displaced?
- Career planning in an AI-transformed world

**Hard idea:** Tasks, not jobs, and the apprenticeship problem. AI changes tasks before it changes whole jobs. The tasks it handles first are often the beginner tasks people learn on, which raises a hard question about how anyone becomes an expert.

**Recent events in this module (last checked October 14, 2026):** Stanford's August 2026 payroll study, Anthropic's March 2026 labor study and January 2026 coding-skills experiment, 2026 job cuts credited to AI, and the June 2026 SAG-AFTRA contract.

## Objectives

By the end, learners can:

1. Distinguish automating a task from automating a whole job, using a job they know.
2. Evaluate a forecast about AI and jobs by its source, method, and time frame.
3. Explain the apprenticeship problem and why entry-level work matters for building expertise.
4. Plan one skill to build that complements AI, and say who gains and who loses as work changes.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Definition for this grade band |
| --- | --- |
| automation | A machine doing a task that a person used to do. |
| augmentation | A tool that makes a person more effective at a task. |
| exposure | How much of a job's tasks an AI system could affect. Exposure is not the same as job loss. |
| displacement | When workers lose jobs or hours to a change in technology. |
| reskilling | Learning new skills to move into different work. |

## Step 1: Hook (explainer, 5 min, free preview)

**Title:** Break a job into tasks

Pick a job you know well. It could be a family member's job, your own part-time job, or a job you want someday.

List ten things a person in that job does in a normal week. Then mark each task with one of three labels:

- AI could do it
- AI could help with it
- AI can't do it

Here is a start for a restaurant manager:

- Write the staff schedule: AI could help
- Calm an upset customer: AI can't
- Train a new server: AI can't
- Write the weekly specials post: AI could do it

[CALLOUT think] Look at your list. How many tasks landed in each column? Did any surprise you?

If your job landed in more than one column, that is the first big idea of this module. Forecasts about AI and work are really about tasks, not whole jobs. A machine doing a task a person used to do is [VOCAB automation]. A tool that makes a person better at a task is [VOCAB augmentation]. Most jobs will see some of each.

The question that matters is what happens to the job when some of its tasks change. Sometimes the job shrinks. Sometimes it grows into something new. In this module you will learn to read the forecasts and look at what is happening so far. Then you will face a hard question the forecasts rarely ask.

## Step 2: Core idea (explainer, 7 min)

**Title:** Reading a forecast

Here are four real kinds of numbers about AI and jobs. They point in different directions.

- **Exposure estimate (2023).** Researchers from OpenAI and the University of Pennsylvania studied language models. About 80% of US workers could have at least 10% of their tasks affected. About 19% could see at least half their tasks affected. The authors said they were not predicting when.
- **Employer survey (2025).** The World Economic Forum projected 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced globally by 2030, a net gain of 78 million.
- **Company announcements (2026).** Block cut 40% of its staff in February 2026, and its CEO pointed to AI. A job-cuts tracker counted about 113,000 cuts tied to AI by late August. Yet executives at Davos told Reuters that AI "would be used as an excuse" for cuts.
- **Measured so far (2025 and 2026).** Yale's Budget Lab found no clear effect on the overall US labor market, most recently in May 2026. In March 2026, Anthropic researchers found "no systematic increase in unemployment" for the most exposed workers.

So which is right? Each answers a different question. [VOCAB exposure] tells you which tasks could be affected, not whether jobs disappear. A survey tells you what managers expect. An announcement tells you what a company wants you to hear. Labor data tells you what has happened, but only so far.

Ask four questions of any forecast:

1. Who published it, and what do they gain from the answer?
2. What did they measure: exposure, expectations, announcements, or real hiring and pay?
3. Over what time frame?
4. What do they mean by "affected"?

## Step 3: The hard idea (explainer, 7 min)

**Title:** Who trains the experts?

Here is a question the big forecasts rarely ask. How does anyone become an expert?

Mostly by doing beginner work. Junior lawyers review documents. Junior coders fix small bugs. New analysts build first drafts. That work teaches them what good looks like. Those beginner tasks are also the kind AI handles well. We will call this the apprenticeship problem.

There are early signs of it. Stanford researchers used payroll data covering millions of US workers. By mid-2026, employment for 22- to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed jobs was 19% below where it would have been. Experienced workers in the same jobs showed no comparable gap. The drop came mostly from less hiring, not layoffs. It showed up where AI replaces work, not where it helps with it. Losing a job or hours to new technology is called [VOCAB displacement].

Learning is at risk too. In a 2026 Anthropic experiment, people learned a new coding tool, some with an AI assistant and some without. On a later quiz, the AI group averaged 50% and the others 67%. The biggest gap was in debugging, the skill that catches mistakes.

But AI can also lift beginners. When a company gave 5,179 support agents an AI assistant, newer agents resolved 34% more issues per hour. So AI might help beginners perform better while they learn less. Which effect wins is an open question.

History offers one hopeful pattern. As ATMs spread from the mid-1990s, banks needed fewer tellers per branch. Branches got cheaper, so banks opened more of them, and teller jobs held up while shifting toward advising customers. When work shifts like this, people need [VOCAB reskilling]: learning new skills to move into different work.

[CALLOUT warning] One pattern from history is not a guarantee. ATMs automated cash handling. AI is aimed at a much wider range of tasks.

[CALLOUT think] If companies stop hiring beginners, where will the senior experts of 2040 come from? Whose job is it to solve that: companies, schools, or governments?

## Step 4: Try it (activity, 10 min)

**Title:** Grade the AI on real tasks

Go back to your job list from Step 1. Pick three tasks: one you marked "AI could do it," one "AI could help," and one "AI can't."

[TRY] Use an AI tool your school allows, or the ARK practice tool once it launches. Ask it to do each of the three tasks, or the closest version it can. For "calm an upset customer," ask it to write what it would say.

Grade each result:

- Usable as is
- Usable after edits
- Not usable

Then answer: What did you need to know to grade it? If you couldn't tell whether the answer was good, that tells you something too.

No AI tool? Your facilitator can share three sample AI answers for the restaurant manager tasks. Grade those instead.

[CALLOUT warning] Use made-up details. Don't type real names, real schedules, or anything personal into an AI tool.

Look at your grades. The knowledge you used to judge the output is the skill that complements AI. A person who can tell a usable draft from a confident mess is worth more with AI, not less. That is also why the apprenticeship problem matters. You build that judgment by doing the work yourself first.

## Step 5: Scenario (scenario, 6 min)

**Title:** The internship

You land a summer internship. Your main task is writing first drafts of weekly reports for your manager. In week two, you notice an AI tool can produce a decent first draft in seconds. (This scenario is made up.)

[SCENARIO] What do you do?

- **A. Say nothing and keep writing every draft by hand.** Feedback: You keep building your own skills, which counts for a lot. But you may look slow, and you miss a chance to learn how the tool fits the work.
- **B. Quietly use the tool and hand in its drafts as your own.** Feedback: This saves time now and may break your workplace's rules. You also skip the practice that turns an intern into someone people trust with harder work.
- **C. Tell your manager, offer to use the tool for first drafts, and spend the saved time checking facts and adding analysis.** Feedback: Strong. You are honest about the tool, you keep the judgment work, and you move toward tasks AI does poorly. Ask your manager what you are allowed to use first.
- **D. Ask for different work, since a machine can do this job.** Feedback: Reasonable if the new work teaches you more. But "a machine can draft this" is not the same as "a machine can do this job." Someone still has to know whether the draft is right.

There is no perfect choice. Notice which ones protect your learning, and which ones only protect your time.

## Step 7: Reflect (reflect, 5 min)

**Title:** One skill this year

**ARK's view:** We think companies that hand beginner work to AI owe the next generation a way in. That could be paid apprenticeships or new junior roles. And while you are learning a skill, do the hard parts yourself, even when AI could do them for you.

**The best case against:** Companies are not schools. Keeping work that AI can do may raise prices and slow progress. New entry-level jobs may appear on their own, as they did after ATMs.

[REFLECT] Do you agree with ARK? What would change your mind?

[REFLECT] Name one skill you will build this year that holds up as AI improves. Why that one, and what is your first step?

Your answers stay on your device. Nobody else will see them.

[DISCUSS]

- The interns debate (from the deck): a CEO says AI gives the same output at a tiny cost. Interns ask how they will ever get hired. A manager asks who becomes tomorrow's senior. Who is right?
- When AI raises productivity, who should share in the gains: owners, workers, customers, or the public? In June 2026, Hollywood actors approved a contract that further limits AI "synthetic" performers. Is bargaining like that the answer?

## Step 8: Recap (recap, 3 min)

[RECAP]

- AI changes tasks before it changes whole jobs. Most jobs mix tasks AI can do, help with, and can't do.
- Forecasts and announcements disagree. Check who made them, what they measured, and over what time frame. Exposure is not job loss.
- The apprenticeship problem: AI handles many beginner tasks, and early data shows less hiring of young workers in exposed jobs.
- Doing a task with AI is not the same as learning it. Judgment complements AI, and you build it by doing the work.
- Gains and losses from AI are uneven, and who shares in them is a choice people make.

Next: you become the builder. How do you design an AI product that helps without causing harm?

## Facilitator guide

**Before you start:**

- Some students have family members who have lost work or worry about losing it. Keep examples about tasks and systems, and never ask whose parent works where.
- Avoid both doom and hype. The honest answer for 2026 is that measured effects are small overall, with early signs for young workers in some jobs.
- Labor numbers change often. Check the Stanford, Yale, and Anthropic pages for newer updates before you teach.
- ARK's view is an opinion, labeled as one. Invite students to argue with it. No grade depends on agreeing.

**Discussion prompts:** Use the deck's interns debate: CEO, interns, manager, customer. Then ask the class which choice in Step 5 they would actually make.

**Answer notes:** Quick check: 1 B, 2 D, 3 B, 4 A, 5 B. Pre/post: 1 B, 2 A, 3 C. If learners choose A on quick check question 5, point out that the AI group still finished the task. The gap was in what they could do afterward.
