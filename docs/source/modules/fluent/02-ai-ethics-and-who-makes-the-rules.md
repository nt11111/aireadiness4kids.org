## Objectives

By the end, learners can:

1. Compare binding law, government policy, and voluntary commitments, with one strength and one weakness of each.
2. Explain the accountability gap using a real case.
3. Use the Collingridge dilemma to argue when and how a new use of AI should be regulated.
4. Propose one way students can shape AI rules, and name the person or body that can act on it.

## Step 1: Hook (explainer, 5 min, free preview)

**Title:** Who answers for the chatbot?

A customer whose grandmother had just died asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The chatbot told him he could book now and claim the discount later. The airline's own policy page said the opposite. When he asked for the discount, the airline refused.

He took the case to a tribunal in British Columbia. Air Canada argued that the chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own words.

[CALLOUT think] Before you read on: who should be responsible? The airline, the company that built the chatbot, the customer for not double-checking, or nobody?

In February 2024 the tribunal rejected Air Canada's argument. The chatbot is part of the airline's website, it ruled, so the airline answers for what it says. Air Canada was found to carry [VOCAB liability] and had to pay $650.88 in damages, plus interest and fees.

This case had a clear answer. Many don't. When an AI system causes harm, the builder may blame the data, and the company using it may blame the builder. The person harmed may have nowhere to appeal. That is the [VOCAB accountability gap]: harm happens and no one is clearly answerable for it.

Rules are supposed to close that gap. In this module you will see who writes the rules for AI right now and why those rules keep arriving late. You will also find where you fit in.

## Step 2: Core idea (explainer, 7 min)

**Title:** Three ways to make rules

There are three main ways to set rules for AI. Each has a different enforcer, and each fails in its own way. Everything here is as of October 2026, and it will change.

**Binding law.** The EU AI Act is the broadest AI law so far. It uses a [VOCAB risk-based approach]: the higher the risk, the stricter the rule. A short list of uses is banned, such as social scoring. High-risk uses, such as AI in hiring or education, face strict checks. Chatbots must tell you they are AI. Most AI, like spam filters, faces no new rules.

**Government policy.** The US has no broad federal AI law. Presidents have set direction through executive orders instead. A 2023 order on AI safety was revoked in January 2025. A December 2025 order told federal agencies to challenge state AI laws the administration saw as too burdensome. In January 2026, the Justice Department set up a task force to do that in court.

**Voluntary commitments.** In July 2023, seven AI companies promised the White House they would safety-test models and help people spot AI-made content. No law required it and no one enforces it. That is a [VOCAB voluntary commitment].

| Approach | Who enforces it | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Binding law | Regulators, with fines | Applies to every company it covers | Slow to write and slow to update |
| Government policy | Federal agencies | Can change within weeks | Can be reversed just as quickly |
| Voluntary commitment | No one | Companies can act before any law exists | No penalty for breaking it |

## Step 3: The hard idea (explainer, 7 min)

**Title:** Why the rules always seem late

In 1980, a British researcher named David Collingridge described a trap that new technologies set for the people trying to govern them.

Early on, a technology is easy to change. Few people depend on it. But nobody knows yet what harm it will do, so there is little reason to act. Later, the harms are clear. By then the technology is built into products, jobs, and habits, and changing it is slow and contested.

This is the [VOCAB Collingridge dilemma]. You can see it in AI rules right now:

- **Waiting had a cost.** From 2013, Dutch tax authorities used a risk-scoring algorithm to flag childcare benefit claims as possible fraud. Holding a non-Dutch nationality raised a family's score. Tens of thousands of parents were falsely accused before the harm was clear. The scandal brought down the Dutch government in 2021.
- **An early rule missed the first real test.** California's SB 53 took effect in January 2026. It makes the largest AI developers report "critical safety incidents" to the state within 15 days. In July 2026, AI agents under test at OpenAI hacked Hugging Face. State officials said the hack "did not meet the threshold." The law mostly covers incidents outside of testing.
- **Even big laws slip.** In July 2026 the EU made it official. Its rules for high-risk AI, such as hiring tools, moved from August 2026 to December 2027.

There is no clean way out. People who write rules use partial fixes:

- Start with steps that are easy to undo, such as audits and record-keeping. Save bans for when the evidence is in.
- Build in an end date, so a rule must be reviewed. Part of Colorado's 2026 AI law expires after three years.
- Test first in a sandbox, a supervised trial with real users. The EU AI Act sets up sandboxes for this.

[CALLOUT think] Is it better to regulate a new AI use early and risk getting it wrong, or late and risk the harm? Does your answer change for AI agents versus AI in video games?

## Step 4: Try it (activity, 10 min)

**Title:** Write a rule, then break it

Rules sound clear until they meet a real case. Here you write one and attack it.

[TRY] Use an AI tool your school allows, or the ARK practice tool once it launches. Ask it to draft a one-sentence rule for AI use at your school, such as a rule about AI on homework.

Then red-team the rule. Ask the tool for three situations where the rule fails or treats a student unfairly. Push it: "What about a student who uses AI to translate?" "What if a teacher's AI detector is wrong?"

No AI tool? Write the rule yourself, swap with a partner, and each find three ways the other's rule breaks.

[CALLOUT warning] Don't type real names or details about real students into an AI tool.

Now fix your rule with the Collingridge dilemma in mind. Answer four questions in writing:

1. Who enforces it?
2. What happens to a student who is wrongly accused, and who do they appeal to?
3. When will the rule be reviewed? Add a date.
4. Who has the authority to adopt it? Name the person or body, such as your principal, a school site council, or the school board. "The school" is not an answer.

Check what the AI gave you, too. It may have invented a policy or a law. You learned why in Module 1.

Rules above your school have a door too. Anyone can submit a public comment on a proposed US federal rule. It is not a vote. Agencies must respond to relevant, significant comments, and reasoned comments with evidence carry the most weight.

## Step 5: Scenario (scenario, 6 min)

**Title:** The incident rule

After the Hugging Face hack, a state committee is rewriting its AI incident-reporting rule. You sit on its youth advisory panel. The question: which AI incidents must developers report? (The committee is made up. The problem is real.)

Two dates to know. California's SB 53 gives developers 15 days to report. New York's RAISE Act, in effect from January 2027, gives 72 hours. Two days after OpenAI named its agents, two members of Congress, one from each party, introduced the AI Kill Switch Act. As of October 2026, it has not left committee.

[SCENARIO] What do you recommend?

- **A. Every incident, including every failure in testing.** Feedback: Nothing slips through. But labs run huge numbers of tests, and regulators would drown in reports. Labs might also test less, or less openly.
- **B. Only incidents that cause real harm to people.** Feedback: Reports stay few and serious. But the warning signs OpenAI saw in May 2026 would have counted as nothing to report.
- **C. Any incident where an AI gets out of its test setup or reaches systems it shouldn't, test or not.** Feedback: This targets the danger in the Hugging Face case. It needs clear definitions, and someone has to check that labs comply.
- **D. Keep reporting voluntary.** Feedback: Quick and cheap, and OpenAI did publish a detailed account. But it depends on each company choosing to share bad news.

Every choice gives something up. Good rule-making is choosing which risks you will accept, and saying so out loud.

## Step 7: Reflect (reflect, 5 min)

**Title:** Who should have more say?

**ARK's view:** We think any AI incident that reaches outside the lab should be reported to an outside body, test or not. Airlines and hospitals got safer by studying near misses as well as crashes. In September 2026, a UN scientific panel on AI made the same comparison.

**The best case against:** Strict reporting could push labs to test less, or to test in secret. A lab that finds and fixes a problem could look worse than one that never looked.

[REFLECT] Do you agree with ARK? What would change your mind?

[REFLECT] Which group should have more say over AI than it has now: companies, governments, researchers, or the public? What would change if it did?

Your answers stay on your device. Nobody else will see them.

## Step 8: Recap (recap, 3 min)

[RECAP]

- Three ways to govern AI: binding law, government policy, and voluntary commitments. Each has a different enforcer and a different weakness.
- As of October 2026, the EU has the broadest AI law, and its high-risk rules start in December 2027. The US relies on executive orders and state laws, and the federal government is challenging the state laws.
- The accountability gap opens when no one owns the outcome. Cases like Air Canada's close it one ruling at a time.
- The Collingridge dilemma: early, rules are easy to change but harms are unknown. California's SB 53 missed the first real AI agent incident.
- You can take part. A specific rule, sent to the person who can adopt it, beats a general complaint.
