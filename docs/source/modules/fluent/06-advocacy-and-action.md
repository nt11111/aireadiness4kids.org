## Objectives

By the end, learners can:

1. Choose a form of advocacy that fits an issue and an audience.
2. Outline a one-page policy brief or op-ed with a clear, specific ask.
3. Write a theory of change for a plan and name the assumption most likely to fail.
4. Present a one-page action plan and give useful feedback to a peer.

## Step 1: Hook (explainer, 5 min, free preview)

**Title:** Who changed a rule?

In the summer of 2020, a high school student in San Jose, California, started a campaign. A statewide ballot measure, Proposition 25, would have replaced cash bail with risk-assessment software. That is the kind of tool you studied in Module 3. Like other opponents, her group argued it would worsen racial inequality.

She and about 15 other teenage volunteers organized town halls, wrote opinion pieces, and ran calls and texts. In November 2020, California voters rejected Proposition 25.

The group kept going. Now called Encode, it helped back California's SB 53 in 2025. A news report described Encode as one of the main drivers of a coalition of more than 20 groups.

That same August, students in England protested a grading algorithm that had lowered close to 40% of A-level grades. Within days, regulators dropped it.

Advocacy also comes from inside. In July 2026, employees of AI companies, including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and Meta, published an open letter called "Pacing the Frontier." They asked the US government to back an international effort to "deliberately pace the frontier." By October, 1,386 had signed.

Nobody handed the students a seat at the table. They used tools anyone can use: writing, organizing, speaking, and building. That is [VOCAB advocacy]: working to change a decision, a rule, or how people think about an issue.

This is the last module. You have spent five modules learning how AI works, who governs it, where it fails, and how to build it well. Now you decide what to do about it.

## Step 2: Core idea (explainer, 7 min)

**Title:** Four ways to act

Advocacy comes in four main forms. Pick the one that fits your audience.

- **Write:** op-eds, policy briefs, and public comments. Best for decision-makers and the public.
- **Organize:** clubs, petitions, and coalitions. Best when numbers matter.
- **Build:** tools that show a better way. Best when people need to see it working.
- **Speak:** testimony, talks, and meetings. Best when you can get in the room.

An [VOCAB op-ed] is an opinion article by someone outside a news outlet's staff. A [VOCAB policy brief] goes straight to a decision-maker. A good brief has four parts:

1. **The problem:** one sentence, with one real example.
2. **The stakes:** who is harmed, and how much.
3. **The evidence:** two or three solid sources beat twenty weak ones.
4. **The ask:** a specific action, by a specific person, with a date.

[VOCAB the ask] is the part most people get wrong. "Someone should do something" is not an ask.

[SORT] Strong or weak ask? | Strong; Weak

- "Someone should do something about AI." -> Weak -> No actor, no action, no deadline.
- "Ban all AI in schools forever." -> Weak -> Too broad to ever pass.
- "Adopt a deepfake policy by June." -> Strong -> Specific, doable, and dated.
- "AI is bad and everyone knows it." -> Weak -> A feeling is not an argument.
- "Principal Ortiz, will you add an AI section to the handbook before next semester?" -> Strong -> It names the person, the action, and the time.

Recall the public comment rule from Module 2: it is not a vote. One well-reasoned comment with evidence can count for more than a hundred copies of the same message.

## Step 3: The hard idea (explainer, 7 min)

**Title:** What has to be true?

Every plan has a hidden theory inside it. "If we write an op-ed, the board will change the policy" assumes three things. The board reads op-eds. The board cares about public opinion on this issue. The board has the authority to change the policy. If any one is false, the plan fails, however well you write.

A [VOCAB theory of change] makes that hidden theory visible. The evaluator Carol Weiss made the term popular in 1995. She described it as the set of assumptions that link a program's small steps to its long-term goal.

Write yours in one line: If we do this, then that will happen, and here is why.

Here is a made-up example.

- **Goal:** the district adopts a policy on AI-made fake images of students.
- **Plan:** collect 300 student signatures, then speak at public comment.
- **Theory:** if 300 students sign, the board will put the policy on its agenda.
- **Assumptions:** the board responds to petitions. 300 is enough to matter. The board, not the state, controls this policy.
- **Weakest assumption:** the first one. Nobody has checked.
- **Cheapest test:** email one board member and ask how items get on the agenda. Cost: one email.

Now a real one. OpenAI named its agents in the Hugging Face hack on July 21, 2026. Two days later, two members of Congress, one from each party, introduced the AI Kill Switch Act. Their bet: a public scare opens a window for a law. By October 2026 the bill had 11 cosponsors, and it had not left a subcommittee. What else has to be true for it to pass?

The deck's "In or Out?" debate shows four strategies. Inside: join the committee. Outside: push with petitions and press. Build: make the better tool. Teach: change one class at a time. The right one depends on which assumptions hold where you live.

[CALLOUT think] Pick a change you'd like to see at your school. What is the one assumption that, if false, sinks your whole plan?

## Step 4: Try it (activity, 10 min)

**Title:** Argue with your own ask

A plan gets stronger when someone tries to knock it down. Here an AI tool plays the skeptic.

[TRY] Write your ask in one sentence. Use an AI tool your school allows, or the ARK practice tool once it launches. Give it this prompt: "You are a busy, skeptical school board member. Give me the three strongest reasons to say no to this request: [your ask]." Then ask: "What would change your mind?"

Sort each objection into one of three piles:

- **Real:** fix your plan.
- **Answerable:** add evidence or a story to your brief.
- **Wrong:** the AI got a fact or a rule wrong. You learned why it can in Module 1. Check before you trust it.

No AI tool? A partner plays the board member and pushes back for three minutes.

[CALLOUT warning] Use a role, like "board member," not a real person's name. Don't type personal information into an AI tool.

The AI's objections are input, not a verdict. You decide what to change. Then rewrite your ask so it survives the best objection.

## Step 5: Scenario (scenario, 6 min)

**Title:** Three minutes at the school board

Your school board is voting on a new AI policy. You signed up for three minutes of public comment. (Rules for public comment vary by district, so check yours.)

[SCENARIO] How do you open?

- **A. A short story about a real situation at your school, shared with permission and without names.** Feedback: Strong. A story gives the board something to picture, and it earns attention for your ask. Follow it with your evidence and your ask.
- **B. A statistic.** Feedback: Evidence matters, but a number alone gives the board nothing to picture. Put it second, right after a story.
- **C. Your ask, in one sentence.** Feedback: Also strong. The board knows from the first moment what you want. Repeat it at the end, so it is the last thing they hear.
- **D. A criticism of the board.** Feedback: You may be right, but people who feel attacked stop listening. Criticize the policy, not the people.

From the deck's "Present Like a Pro": open with the story, not the statistics. Make one ask per audience, and say it twice. End with the first step, not a thank-you.

## Step 7: Reflect (reflect, 5 min)

**Title:** Your action plan

**ARK's view:** We think students belong in the room when schools write AI rules. Stanford's 2026 AI Index found that four in five US high school and college students use AI for school. Only 6% of teachers said their school's policies were clear. The people using a tool most should help write its rules.

**The best case against:** Students change every year, and adults carry the legal duty for school safety. A student seat can also become a token that lets adults say they listened.

[REFLECT] Do you agree with ARK? What would change your mind?

[REFLECT] Which form of advocacy fits you best right now: writing, organizing, building, or speaking? What would it take to try a different one?

Your answers stay on your device. Nobody else will see them.

Now the capstone. Fill in a one-page action plan:

1. **The issue:** one sentence, with one example.
2. **The decision-maker:** a name or role, not "the government."
3. **The ask:** specific and dated.
4. **Your theory of change:** if we do this, then that will happen, and here is why.
5. **The assumption to test first,** and how you will test it.
6. **Allies:** two people who will join you this month.
7. **First step:** something you can do this week.

Present your plan to a peer in two minutes. Your peer answers three questions:

- Could you repeat the ask after hearing it once?
- Is the decision-maker the person who can actually say yes?
- What is the weakest assumption, and did the plan test it?

## Step 8: Recap (recap, 3 min)

[RECAP]

- Advocacy has four forms: write, organize, build, and speak. Pick the one that fits your audience.
- Lead with a clear ask: a specific action, by a specific person, with a date.

**Before you start:**

- Students choose their own issues. Help them narrow broad issues to one decision-maker and one ask.
- Some issues touch politics. Keep the focus on the method: the ask, the evidence, and the theory of change. Don't tell students which side to take. The Kill Switch Act has sponsors from both parties, and students can argue for or against it.
- ARK's view is an opinion, labeled as one. Invite students to argue with it. No grade depends on agreeing.
- If a student's issue involves harm to a real person, such as fake images, steer the plan toward policy and support systems, never toward naming anyone.

**Discussion prompts:** Use the deck's "In or Out?" debate (inside, outside, build, teach) and the "What bugs you?" poll to help students pick issues.

**Answer notes:** Quick check: 1 B, 2 C, 3 B, 4 C. Pre/post: 1 B, 2 A, 3 B. If learners pick A on quick check question 2, point out that a fact is evidence, not an ask.
