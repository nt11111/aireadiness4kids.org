**Module 4: Deepfakes & Misinformation****
** Course: AI Literate (6-8). Writer: Rishi. Peer checker: Neil.
 Status: Writing

On the site: Students learn to spot AI-generated content and think critically about media they encounter online. Slide deck

Key topics:

- What are deepfakes and how are they made?
- How to spot AI-generated images, video, and audio
- Why people create and spread misinformation
- Tools and habits for fact-checking

**Objectives****
** By the end, learners can:

- Explain what a deepfake is and, in general terms, how AI produces one.
- Question an image, video, or audio clip using a short checklist.
- Give two reasons people create and spread false content.
- Use three fact-checking moves: stop, check the source, find a second source.

**Vocabulary**

| Term | Definition for this grade band |
| --- | --- |
| deepfake | AI-made or AI-altered video, image, or audio that shows a real person doing or saying something they did not. |
| misinformation | False information shared by someone who does not know it is false. |
| disinformation | False information spread on purpose. |
| lateral reading | Leaving a page to see what other trusted sources say about it. |

-----

**Step 1: Hook** (explainer, 5 min, free preview)
 Title: Did they really say that?

Student council elections are next week. Then a video lands in your group chat.

In it, Ethan Brooks, one of the candidates, seems to say he doesn't care what students think. He also seems to promise he'll cancel several popular school events if he wins. The video looks real, and the voice sounds just like him.

Friends are upset. Someone shares it. Then someone else does. Within hours, it has spread across the grade.

[CALLOUT think] Before you forward it, is it real? How would you check?

Here's what the chat doesn't know. Ethan never said any of this. His opponent, Marcus Lee, made the clip with AI and posted it as if it were real. He wanted students to turn on Ethan. Ethan and Marcus are made up.

That makes it a [VOCAB deepfake]. A deepfake is AI-made or AI-altered video, image, or audio. It shows a real person doing or saying something they didn't. [CITATION NEEDED]

This module covers how fakes are made, why people share them, and what to do before you do.

-----

**Step 2: Core idea** (explainer, 7 min)
 Title: How fakes are made, and what gives them away

How is a fake made? An AI tool studies lots of real photos, videos, and voices. It learns what faces look like and how voices sound. Then it can make new images, video, or audio that seem real. [CITATION NEEDED]

[CALLOUT fact] As of 2026, clues like odd hands are getting less reliable. AI tools keep improving.

That changes what to look for. A fake can look perfect. So where a clip came from matters more than how it looks.

Think back to Ethan's video. It looked real. What would help you more: studying his face, or finding out who posted it first?

[SORT] How much should each clue change your mind? | Strong clue; Weak clue

- Only one account posted it, and that account is new -> Strong clue -> A new account gives you no history to trust.
- You can't find a second trusted source that backs it up -> Strong clue -> If no trusted source confirms it, treat it as unconfirmed.
- A search for the original clip shows it said something different -> Strong clue -> Comparing shows what changed.
- The hands look odd -> Weak clue -> AI tools improve, and real photos can look odd too.
- The video looks completely real -> Weak clue -> A flawless look proves nothing.
- An AI detector tool says it's real or fake -> Weak clue -> Detector tools aren't always reliable.

-----

**Step 3: Why fakes spread** (explainer, 7 min)
 Title: Why fakes spread

Go back to Ethan's clip. Marcus made it knowing it was false. That's [VOCAB disinformation]: false information spread on purpose.

Now say a friend forwards it, sure it's real. That's [VOCAB misinformation]: false information shared by someone who doesn't know it's false.

One clip, two people. Marcus knew. Your friend didn't.

Why would someone make a fake on purpose? People have many reasons. Here are two.

Win an advantage. Marcus wanted to win an election. Fakes can be used to damage a rival.

Make money or get attention. Shocking posts get clicks and views, and some people profit from that.

Why do the rest of us share? Fakes often make us feel something strong, like surprise or anger. That feeling can make us share without thinking.

[CALLOUT think] Who gains if the Ethan clip spreads? Who gets hurt?

[SORT] Did the sharer know it was false? | Disinformation; Misinformation

- Marcus makes a fake clip to hurt Ethan -> Disinformation -> He knew, and he did it on purpose.
- A friend forwards the clip, sure it's real -> Misinformation -> They didn't know it was false.
- A page posts fake shocking videos to get views -> Disinformation -> The page knows they're false and posts them for clicks.
- You share the clip, then learn it's fake -> Misinformation -> You believed it was real when you shared it.

-----

**Step 4: Check before you share** (explainer, 7 min)
 Title: Check before you share

Before you share a clip, use three moves. They work on images, video, and audio.

Stop. How does the clip make you feel? A strong feeling is a signal to pause before you share.

Check the source. Who posted it first, and what is their history? Who gains if you believe it?

Find a second source. Does a source you trust confirm it? If not, treat it as unconfirmed.

[FIGURE] Three steps in a row, left to right: a raised hand labeled Stop, a magnifying glass over an account name labeled Check the source, two browser tabs side by side labeled Find a second source | Alt text: Diagram of three steps in order: stop, check the source, find a second source.

The third move uses [VOCAB lateral reading]: leaving a page to see what other trusted sources say about it. Open a new tab and search for the claim. Don't keep reading the page that gave it to you.

For a video, take a screenshot and run a reverse image search. It can lead you to the original. For audio, search for the quote and see who else reports it.

[CALLOUT tip] Try it on Ethan's clip. Who posted it first? Do other trusted sources, like Ethan's own account or the school newspaper, say he said it?

[CALLOUT warning] Odd details can make you pause, but a clean clip proves nothing. If you can't confirm it, don't pass it on.

**Step 5: Scenario** (scenario, 6 min)
 Title: The group chat

A clip is going around your grade's group chats. It sounds like Jasper saying he got the answers to Friday's test. He says he'll send them to anyone who asks. Jasper is made up.

The clip came from an account you don't recognize. Nothing from your teacher or school mentions it. Some students forward it to call Jasper a cheater. Others pass it on, hoping to get the answers. Jasper tells you he never said it.

[SCENARIO] What would you do?

- A. Forward it so others can hear it. Feedback: This spreads a clip no one has confirmed. If it's fake, Jasper is blamed for words he never said. There are no real answers to get.
- B. Ignore it. Feedback: You don't add to the spread, which helps a little. But Jasper is still alone with the clip, and others keep passing it on.
- C. Reply in the chat that it's fake. Feedback: Speaking up can help Jasper. Back it up with what you checked, like an account you don't know and no trusted source. A flat "it's fake" may not convince anyone.
- D. Tell an adult and report the clip. Feedback: A strong choice. An adult can support Jasper and help report it. You can do your checks first, then add what you found.

Follow-up: Which of the three moves would you do first, and what would you look for?

**Step 6: Quick check** (check, 4 min)
 Title: Quick check

[CHECK] Which best describes a deepfake?
 A. A real video with a filter on it
 B. AI-made media of a real person, built by learning from many real examples
 C. Any video that goes viral
 D. A video posted by a fake account
 Answer: B. The AI studies real photos, videos, or voices. Then it makes media that shows a real person saying or doing something they never did.

[CHECK] A shocking clip is spreading. Which clue should change your mind most?
 A. The hands look odd
 B. The video looks real
 C. A detector tool says it's real
 D. No trusted source backs it up
 Answer: D. A missing second source is a strong clue. Looks and detectors are weak clues.

[CHECK] Which are two reasons people make false content on purpose?
 A. To win an advantage, or to make money or get attention
 B. To help friends, or to teach a lesson
 C. To fix a mistake, or to share news fast
 D. To follow a rule, or to finish homework
 Answer: A. Fakes can damage a rival or bring clicks and attention.

[CHECK] Which list shows the three moves in order?
 A. Share, check, delete
 B. Check the source, share, find a second source
 C. Stop, check the source, find a second source
 D. Find a second source, stop, share
 Answer: C. Stop first, check who posted it, then find a second trusted source.

**Step 7: Reflect** (reflect, 5 min)
 Title: What I almost shared

[REFLECT] Think of a time you believed something online that turned out to be false, or nearly shared it. What made it feel true? Which of the three moves could have helped?

This stays on your device. Nobody else will see it.

[DISCUSS]

- Who gains when a fake spreads, and who pays the price?
- Why might a surprising or upsetting story get shared more than a calm one?
- Why might someone share a clip to fit in with a group, even before checking it?

-----

**Step 8: Recap** (recap, 3 min)
 Title: Recap

[RECAP]

- AI can make fakes that look and sound real.
- The source matters more than the pixels. A flawless clip can still be fake.
- Use three moves: stop, check the source, find a second source.
- If you can't confirm a clip, don't pass it on.

Next: Privacy & Your Data. What data do AI apps collect, and how can you protect yours?

**Pre/post check****
** The same three questions open and close the module.

[CHECK] Which one is a deepfake?
 A. A photo with a funny filter
 B. A video clip cut shorter to fit a screen
 C. AI-made audio of a real person saying words they never said
 D. A post with lots of shares
 Answer: C. A deepfake uses AI to make a real person seem to say or do something they never did.

[CHECK] Which is the strongest sign a clip may be fake?
 A. Only one account posted it, and no trusted source backs it up
 B. The hands and lighting look a little odd
 C. It looks so real that it must be real
 D. A detector tool says it is probably real
 Answer: A. The source matters more than how a clip looks.

[CHECK] A shocking clip lands in your group chat. What should you do first?
 A. Forward it so friends can decide
 B. Reply that it's fake
 C. Delete the whole chat
 D. Stop, check who posted it, then look for a second source
 Answer: D. Pause first. Then check the source and find a second trusted source.

**Facilitator guide**

Materials:

- The six clue cards from Step 2, printed or on index cards, plus two labeled spots: Strong clue and Weak clue

Before you start (sensitive spots, what to avoid):

- Use only the invented clips in this module. Don't show or link a real deepfake, and don't make one with learners.
- Ethan, Marcus, and Jasper are made up. Don't name real classmates, teachers, or public figures in examples. If a learner has one of these names, swap it.
- Some learners may have been the target of a fake, or may have shared one. Reflect is private, so don't ask anyone to read theirs aloud or say what they shared. Point any learner who wants to talk to a trusted adult.
- Keep the tone calm. The goal is habits, not fear.
- Step 5 mentions cheating on a test. Keep the focus on the habit of checking, not on who cheats.
- Many AI tools require users to be 13 or older, and these learners are 11 to 13. Don't tell anyone to try a tool. Any demo is led by you.
- The clue labels are for today. As of 2026, visual clues are weak, but tools change, so tell learners the habits matter most.
- Name AI products only as examples. Don't recommend a detector or any other tool.

Discussion prompts: use the three in Step 7. For extra time, try: "Should apps label AI-made clips? What are the pros and cons?" Show both sides and let learners decide.

Answer notes:

- Step 1: There is no single answer. Steer toward who posted it first and whether a trusted source confirms it.
- Step 2 sort: Cards 1 to 3 are strong clues: one new account, no second trusted source, and an original that says something different. The hands, the "looks completely real" card, and the detector tool are weak. Ask learners to defend their picks, and remind them these labels are for today.
- Step 3 sort: Marcus's clip is disinformation. The friend forwarding it is misinformation. The page posting fakes for clicks is disinformation. Sharing a fake you believed is misinformation. Ask: can one clip be both? Yes. It can start as disinformation and become misinformation when others share it believing it's real.
- Step 5: D is the strongest choice. A spreads an unconfirmed clip. B and C each help partly. Ask what learners would check first.
- Step 6 answers: B, D, A, C.
- Pre/post check answers: C, A, D.

## Depth pass: suggested upgrades (October 14, 2026)

From a review of all three courses. Nothing above this heading was changed. Rishi decides what to keep, then deletes this section. Claude opened every source below on October 14, 2026.

**Overall:** This is the strongest AI Literate module. The point that "the source matters more than the pixels" is right, and it will hold up. Two additions would back it with evidence and tell students what help exists.

### 1. Big idea for the header

**Big idea:** You can't trust your eyes anymore. Trust the trail: who posted it first, and who confirms it.

### 2. Evidence for "clues like odd hands are less reliable" (Step 2)

[CALLOUT fact] In a 2025 study, about 12,500 people judged roughly 287,000 images as real or AI-made. They were right 62% of the time. Guessing would get 50%.

[CALLOUT think] If people are only a little better than guessing, what should you check instead of the picture?

Source: Microsoft AI for Good Lab study (arXiv, July 2025) ->[https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18640v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18640v1)

Note: the people in this study chose to take the quiz, so they may not match everyone. Don't call it "pure chance." It was a bit better than chance.

### 3. What help exists (Step 4 or the facilitator guide)

Some middle schools have had cases of students using AI to make fake nude images of classmates. Learners should know this is serious, and that help exists. Suggested wording:

[CALLOUT warning] Someone might use AI to make a fake nude image of a real person, even a classmate. Don't share it. Tell a trusted adult right away. Since May 19, 2026, big US platforms must remove these images within 48 hours of a valid request.

Source: FTC begins enforcing the TAKE IT DOWN Act (May 2026) ->[https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-begins-enforcing-take-it-down-act](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-begins-enforcing-take-it-down-act)

Facilitator note: keep this to one calm sentence with learners. Don't describe cases.

### 4. A harder check question

[CHECK] A video has a label saying it was made with AI. Someone crops the label off and reposts it. What does this show?

A. Labels make fakes impossible

B. Labels help, but they can be removed, so checking the source still matters

C. The video is now real

D. Only bad apps use labels

*Answer: B. A label is one clue. The trail of who posted it first is still your best check.*

### 5. ARK's view (new in guide 1.4)

Suggested for Step 7, before the reflect prompt:

**ARK's view:** We think makers of realistic AI video and voice tools should label what they make. The labels should be hard to strip. Platforms should show those labels. Labels will never be enough, so the checking habit matters most.

**The best case against:** Labels can be faked or removed. They might even make people trust anything without a label.

[REFLECT] Do you agree with ARK? What would change your mind?
