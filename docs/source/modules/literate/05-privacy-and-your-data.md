ON THE SITE

Students explore how AI companies collect data, what they do with it, and how students can protect themselves. Slide deck

KEY TOPICS

- What data AI apps collect about users

- Data brokers and how personal information is sold

- Reading privacy policies (and why almost no one does)

- Practical steps: settings, permissions, and habits

OBJECTIVES

By the end of this module, students can:

1. List the kinds of data AI apps collect.

2. Explain in simple terms how personal data gets bought and sold.

3. Find three answers in a privacy policy: what is collected, who gets it, and how to delete it.

4. Change two settings or habits to protect their data.

VOCABULARY

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| personal data | Information that can be tied to you, such as your name, location, contacts, or what you type. |
| data broker | A company that collects information about people and sells it. |
| privacy policy | A document where a company says what data it collects and what it does with it. |
| permission | Access you give an app, such as to your camera, contacts, or location. |

STEP 2: Where your data goes

Type: explainer

Minutes: 7

Your data takes a trip. Follow it through four stops.

Collected. An app gathers data when you use it.

Used. A company may use it to target ads or train AI.

Shared. Some apps share data with partners and advertisers.

Sold. A [VOCAB data broker] is a company that collects information about people and sells it.

[FIGURE] Four boxes in a row joined by arrows: a phone labeled Collected, a gear labeled Used, two overlapping circles labeled Shared, a price tag labeled Sold | Alt text: Diagram of data moving from your phone to use, then sharing, then sale.

[CALLOUT fact] As of 2026, AI companies handle data differently. Some train on what you share unless you opt out.

[CALLOUT think] Ask this each time: does the app need that data to work?

[SORT] Does the app need this data to work? | Needed; Not needed

- A map app and your location -> Needed -> It must know where you are.

- A video call app and your camera -> Needed -> The call needs your video.

- A photo editing app and the photo you pick -> Needed -> It can't edit a photo it can't see.

- A weather app and your city -> Needed -> It needs a place to forecast.

- A food delivery app and your address -> Needed -> The driver must find you.

- A voice assistant and your microphone -> Needed -> It must hear you to answer.

- A flashlight app and your contacts -> Not needed -> A flashlight never uses your contacts.

- Instagram and your contacts -> Not needed -> It works without them. Uploading is your choice.

STEP 3: Read the policy

Type: explainer

Minutes: 7

A [VOCAB privacy policy] is a document where a company says what data it collects and what it does with it. Many people skip them. A 2023 Pew survey found 56% of U.S. adults often click "agree" without reading them.

You don't have to read every word. Look for three answers.

What is collected? Look for "information we collect."

Who gets it? Look for words like "share" and "partners."

How do I delete it? Look for "delete," "your choices," or "your rights."

[CALLOUT tip] Search the page for "share" or "delete" to jump to the right part.

[CALLOUT fact] As of 2026, a U.S. law called COPPA covers kids under 13. Many apps need a parent's consent to collect their data.

[SORT] Which question does each line answer? | What is collected; Who gets it; How to delete it

- "We collect your location when the app is open." -> What is collected -> It names the data.

- "We collect your username and the levels you play." -> What is collected -> It names the data.

- "We share data with companies that help us improve the app." -> Who gets it -> Other companies get your data.

- "We may share your activity with advertising partners." -> Who gets it -> Partners get your data.

- "Choose Delete Account in Settings to remove your data." -> How to delete it -> It gives the steps.

- "Email us to ask us to erase your information." -> How to delete it -> It tells you how to ask.

STEP 4: Protect your data

Type: explainer

Minutes: 7

You can take back some control. Start with three kinds of change.

Permissions. A [VOCAB permission] is access you give an app, such as to your camera, contacts, or location. Open your phone's settings and look at each app's permissions. Turn off any the app doesn't need.

Settings. Look for choices about location, ads, and who sees your posts. Pick the most private choice you're comfortable with.

Habits. Delete apps you don't use. Pause before you upload your contacts or photos.

[CALLOUT tip] As of 2026, many phones offer choices like "while using the app" or "never" for location. Pick the narrowest one that still works.

[CALLOUT warning] Deleting an app doesn't always delete your account. To remove your data, delete the account first.

[CALLOUT think] Pick two changes you could make this week. Keep your picks to yourself. On a shared or family device, ask a parent or guardian first.

Sources:

- https://privacy.ca.gov/2025/09/limit-mobile-app-tracking/

- https://www.consumerreports.org/electronics-computers/privacy/protect-your-privacy-from-the-apps-on-your-phone-a1049648633/

STEP 5: The quiz app

Type: scenario

Minutes: 6

A friend sends you a quiz link: "Which animal are you?" The app says it will match your face to an animal. Before it shows your result, it asks for three things: your camera, your contacts, and your microphone. The app is made up.

[SCENARIO] What would you do?

- A. Allow all three so you can see your result. Feedback: You'd see your animal, but you'd also give away your contacts and microphone. Contacts hold your friends' names and numbers, and they never agreed to share them.

- B. Allow only the camera, since the quiz needs a selfie. Feedback: A strong choice. The quiz needs your camera. It has no use for your contacts or microphone, so those stay private. A selfie is still personal data, so check where it goes.

- C. Skip the quiz. Feedback: This gives away nothing, and that's a fine choice. You miss a bit of fun, though. Checking the policy first could help you decide.

- D. Ask a parent or guardian. Feedback: A good move. An adult can help you read the policy and decide what to allow. Many apps have extra rules for users under 13.

Follow-up: Which of the three policy questions would you check first?

Sources:

- No new sources. The under 13 line rests on the COPPA source in Step 3.
