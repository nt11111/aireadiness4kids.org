**Module 3: AI & Schoolwork: Where's the Line?****
** Course: AI Literate (6-8). Writer: Rishi. Peer checker: Neil.
 Status: Writing

On the site: Students examine how AI tools like ChatGPT are changing what it means to do school assignments, and where responsible use ends and academic dishonesty begins. Slide deck

Key topics:

- How students are currently using AI for homework
- Plagiarism vs. AI assistance: what is and isn't allowed?
- When AI use helps learning vs. when it undermines it
- School policies and why they exist

**Objectives****
** By the end, learners can:

- Describe three ways students use AI for schoolwork.
- Tell the difference between getting help and handing over the thinking.
- Apply the explain-it-back test to a real assignment: could I explain this without the AI?
- Explain why schools set AI rules and how to find the rule for a class.

**Vocabulary**

| Term | Definition for this grade band |
| --- | --- |
| generative AI | AI that produces new text, images, or audio from a prompt. |
| plagiarism | Presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own. |
| academic honesty | Doing your own work and being open about the help you got. |
| disclosure | Saying when and how you used a tool or a source. |

**Step 1: Hook** (explainer, 5 min, free preview)
 Title: Two students, one essay

Meet two students with the same essay assignment. Both open the same AI tool. It's [VOCAB generative AI], which means AI that makes new text, images, or audio from a prompt.

The first student writes a rough draft alone. Then they ask the tool for feedback and fix the weak parts.

The second student pastes in the assignment. They turn in whatever comes back.

[CALLOUT think] Which student crossed a line? Why is that harder to say than it looks?

Both students used the same tool, and both got help. So the line can't be about the tool. This module is about where it is.

-----

**Step 2: Core idea** (explainer, 7 min)
 Title: Help vs. handing it over

AI help runs along a range. Brainstorming and explaining a concept come first. Next is feedback on your draft. Then comes rewriting your sentences. Last is writing the whole thing.

[CALLOUT tip] Try the explain-it-back test: could I explain this without the AI? If yes, you probably learned. If no, the tool did the thinking.

[VOCAB plagiarism] means presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own. [VOCAB academic honesty] means doing your own work and being open about help. [VOCAB disclosure] means saying when you used a tool.

One reason for AI rules: class is practice, and teachers need to see what you can do. Classes practice different skills, so rules differ. Check the assignment directions or ask your teacher.

[SORT] Where does each use belong? | Helps you learn; Skips the learning; Depends on the class rule

- Asking an AI tool to quiz you on a chapter -> Helps you learn -> You practice recalling it.
- Having an AI tool make flashcards from your notes -> Helps you learn -> You still do the studying.
- Asking an AI tool for feedback on your own draft -> Helps you learn -> The writing is still yours.
- Pasting the assignment into an AI tool and submitting the result -> Skips the learning -> You couldn't explain it back.
- Having an AI tool solve your math homework -> Skips the learning -> You skip the practice.
- Using an AI tool to brainstorm essay topics -> Depends on the class rule -> Some teachers want your ideas first.
- Asking an AI tool to fix grammar in your finished draft -> Depends on the class rule -> Some classes grade editing too.
- Using an AI tool to outline, then writing from it -> Depends on the class rule -> Some classes grade planning too.

**Step 3: Scenario** (scenario, 6 min)
 Title: The night before the test

Your science test on the water cycle is tomorrow. You read the chapter once, and it didn't stick. It's 9 p.m., and you have an AI tool open.

[SCENARIO] What would you do?

- A. Ask the tool to quiz you on the chapter, then check each answer against your notes. Feedback: A strong choice. The tool makes you practice recalling the material, and your notes let you check its answers. Practicing recall helps you explain it back.
- B. Ask the tool to guess what's on the test and study only those answers. Feedback: The tool can't see your test. You'd learn a few answers and miss the rest. You probably couldn't explain them back.
- C. Ask the tool for a summary and skip rereading the chapter. Feedback: A summary saves time. It also gives you the tool's view of what matters, and your teacher may see it differently. Reading and explaining it yourself teaches more.
- D. Message your teacher to ask if an AI tool is okay for practice. Study from your notes while you wait. Feedback: A smart move. Rules differ by class, and your teacher can tell you the rule. Studying meanwhile keeps you practicing.

Follow-up: What would you change if your teacher said no AI tools for this class?

**Step 4: Quick check** (check, 4 min)
 Title: Quick check

[CHECK] Which list shows three ways students use AI to help them learn?
 A. Pasting in the assignment, solving math homework, writing a report
 B. Quizzing yourself, pasting in the assignment, brainstorming
 C. Quizzing yourself, making flashcards, getting feedback on your draft
 D. Making flashcards, writing a report, getting feedback on your draft
 Answer: C. All three keep you doing the thinking. A skips the learning. B and D each include one use that skips it.

[CHECK] Which result passes the explain-it-back test?
 A. Your answers match the tool's
 B. You can explain each answer without the tool
 C. You finished in half the time
 D. The tool called your work good
 Answer: B. The test asks if you could explain it without the AI. The other results don't show that.

[CHECK] You aren't sure if an AI tool is allowed for a project. What should you do first?
 A. Use it and say nothing
 B. Copy a friend's class rule
 C. Assume it's fine
 D. Check the directions or ask your teacher
 Answer: D. Rules differ by class, so the directions or your teacher can tell you the rule.

[CHECK] Why might two classes have different AI rules?
 A. They practice different skills
 B. Teachers never talk
 C. AI works in some subjects only
 D. The rules are random
 Answer: A. Each class practices different skills, so what counts as help changes.

**Step 5: Reflect** (reflect, 5 min)
 Title: Where I use it

[REFLECT] Think of one assignment where an AI tool helped you learn, or could have. Then think of one where it would have skipped the learning. What was the difference? Could you explain each one back without the tool?

This stays on your device. Nobody else will see it.

[DISCUSS]

- If you used an AI tool to study, should you tell your teacher? Why?
- Can the same use of AI be fine in one class and not in another? Why?
- What would a fair AI rule for our grade say, and who should decide it?

**Step 6: Recap** (recap, 3 min)
 Title: Recap

[RECAP]

- AI can help you learn or replace the learning. The difference is who does the thinking.
- Try the explain-it-back test: could I explain this without the AI?
- Rules differ by class, so check the directions or ask your teacher.
- When you use a tool, say how you used it. That's called disclosure.

**Facilitator guide**

Materials:

- The eight sort cards from Step 2, printed or on index cards
- Three labeled spots in the room or on the board: Helps you learn, Skips the learning, Depends on the class rule
- A screen or projector for the scenario
- The pre/post check, printed or on a device, using the same copy at the start and the end
- Paper or a device for Reflect (stays private)
- Optional: a sample assignment sheet from your class, to practice finding the AI rule

Timing for a 60-90 minute class:

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Step 1: Hook | 8 | 10 | Read the two students, take a vote on who crossed a line, then ask why it's hard to say. |
| Step 2: Core idea | 15 | 22 | Walk through the range, teach the explain-it-back test, then run the card sort in small groups. |
| Step 3: Scenario | 10 | 18 | Learners vote on A to D, then talk through the follow-up. |
| Step 4: Quick check | 5 | 8 | Answer the four questions, then go over each explanation. |
| Step 5: Reflect | 10 | 17 | Private writing first, then the discussion prompts. |
| Step 6: Recap and post-check | 7 | 10 | Read the recap, repeat the three pre-check questions, and compare. |

Before you start (sensitive spots, what to avoid):

- AI rules differ by school and by teacher. Do not tell learners what their school allows. Tell them how to find out: the assignment directions, the class handout, or their teacher.
- Don't ask learners to say whether they have used AI on a real assignment. Reflect is private, so invite opinions about rules instead of confessions.
- The three "depends" cards are meant to be debated. Don't settle them with a verdict.
- The scenario and examples are made up. Don't ask learners to sign up for or try any AI tool. Check a tool's age rules before anyone uses it.
- Name AI products only as examples. Don't recommend one.

Discussion prompts: use the three in Step 5. For extra time, return to the Step 1 question: which student crossed a line, and why is it hard to say?

Answer notes:

- Step 1: There is no single right answer. Both students used the same tool, so steer toward who did the thinking and the explain-it-back test.
- Step 2 sort: Cards 1, 2, and 4 go in "helps." Cards 5 and 6 go in "skips." Cards 9, 10, and 11 go in "depends." For the "depends" cards, ask what would make each one okay and who decides.
- Step 3: A and D are the strongest choices. B and C cost more. For the follow-up, learners can say they'd still quiz themselves from notes or make flashcards by hand.
- Step 4 answers: C, B, D, A.
- Pre/post check answers: B, D, A.

## Depth pass: suggested upgrades (October 14, 2026)

From a review of all three courses. Nothing above this heading was changed. Rishi decides what to keep, then deletes this section. Claude opened every source below on October 14, 2026.

**Overall:** The explain-it-back test is a strong, lasting idea. What the module lacks is evidence. Right now it asks students to trust the rule. A 2025 study backs the rule up. Middle schoolers will find evidence more convincing than a rule.

### 1. Big idea for the header

**Big idea:** Getting the work done is not the same as learning. AI can raise your score today and lower your skill tomorrow.

### 2. Add the evidence to Step 2 (paste-ready)

[CALLOUT fact] In a study of nearly 1,000 high school students in Turkey, some practiced math with a chatbot. When it was taken away, they did 17% worse on a test than students who practiced without one. A version that gave hints, not answers, mostly avoided the drop.

Source: Bastani et al. (PNAS, 2025), via PubMed ->[https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560616/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560616/)

And for Step 1, a number students will recognize:

[CALLOUT fact] In a 2025 Pew survey, 54% of US teens said they had used chatbots for schoolwork. About 1 in 10 said they do all or most of their schoolwork with one.

Source: How Teens Use and View AI (Pew Research Center, February 24, 2026) ->[https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/)

### 3. Add AI detectors, with care

Students get accused by AI detectors, so they should know detectors make mistakes. Don't use a percentage for all detectors. Newer studies disagree. Use this one case instead:

[CALLOUT fact] A company called Workado said its AI detector was 98% accurate. In 2025, the US Federal Trade Commission said independent tests put it at 53% on general writing. That is close to a coin flip.

Source: FTC and Workado (April 28, 2025) ->[https://www.ftc.gov/node/88167](https://www.ftc.gov/node/88167)

### 4. A harder check question

[CHECK] A student uses AI on every practice problem and gets them all right. On the test, with no AI, they struggle. What best explains it?

A. The test was harder than the practice

B. The AI did the thinking during practice, so the student's skill didn't grow

C. The student was unlucky

D. AI makes everyone worse at math

*Answer: B. Practice builds skill only when you do the thinking. The explain-it-back test catches this early.*

### 5. ARK's view (new in guide 1.4)

Suggested for Step 5, before the reflect prompt:

**ARK's view:** We think schools should teach students how to use AI well, instead of just policing it. In a 2025 RAND survey, over 80% of students said teachers had not taught them how. You can't follow a line nobody showed you.

**The best case against:** Class time is limited. Some skills, like writing a first draft, may need practice with no AI at all.

[REFLECT] Do you agree with ARK? What would change your mind?
