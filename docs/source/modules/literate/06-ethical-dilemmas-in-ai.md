Course: AI Literate (6-8)

Status: Writing

ON THE SITE

Students debate real-world AI ethics scenarios, applying a structured decision-making framework. Slide deck

KEY TOPICS

- Introduction to AI ethics: fairness, accountability, transparency

- Scenario 1: AI in hiring decisions

- Scenario 2: AI content moderation on social media

- Scenario 3: AI-powered surveillance in public spaces

- How to reason through ethical trade-offs

OBJECTIVES

By the end, learners can:

1. Define fairness, accountability, and transparency for AI.

2. Work through a case with four questions: who is affected, what could go wrong, who is responsible, and what would make it fairer.

3. Argue both sides of an AI case.

4. Defend a position with reasons.

5. Spot the trade-off in an AI decision.

VOCABULARY

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| ethics | Thinking carefully about what is right, fair, and good. |
| fairness | Treating people in an equal and reasonable way. |
| accountability | Someone is answerable when things go wrong. |
| transparency | People can find out how a decision was made. |
| trade-off | Getting more of one thing by giving up some of another. |

STEP 1: No easy answer

Type: explainer

Minutes: 5

A school is thinking about AI cameras in the lunch line. The cameras would recognize each student and charge their account, so nobody waits to swipe a card. This school is made up.

[CALLOUT think] What is your first reaction? Yes, no, or not sure? Keep it to yourself for now.

Here is one side. Lines move faster, and nobody forgets a card.

Here is the other side. The cameras watch every student, every day, and someone has to store those faces.

Both sides have a point. Many AI choices work this way. You gain something and give something up. That is a [VOCAB trade-off]: getting more of one thing by giving up some of another.

Thinking about what is right, fair, and good is called [VOCAB ethics]. This module gives you a way to think through hard calls, and to explain your choice.

- None. The school and the cameras are made up, and the step says so.

STEP 2: Four questions for hard calls

Type: explainer

Minutes: 7

Three ideas help you judge an AI tool.

[VOCAB fairness]: Treating people in an equal and reasonable way.

[VOCAB accountability]: Someone is answerable when things go wrong.

[VOCAB transparency]: People can find out how a decision was made.

Four questions help you use them.

1. Who is affected?

2. What could go wrong?

3. Who is responsible?

4. What would make it fairer?

[CALLOUT tip] Start with question 1. You can't judge a tool until you know who it touches.

Try them on a made up case. A grocery chain gets 500 job applications. It uses a tool to rank the resumes. The tool learned from past hires, and most of them came from one group. So it ranks people like them higher.

Who is affected? Everyone who applies, and the store.

What could go wrong? Strong applicants from other groups get ranked low and are never seen.

Who is responsible? The chain that chose the tool, and the company that built it.

What would make it fairer? A person reviews the low ranked resumes, and the chain tells applicants it uses a tool.

[CALLOUT think] Which of the three ideas does this tool put at risk? Pick one and say why.

Sources:

- None. The grocery chain and the tool are made up.

STEP 3: The post that got taken down

Type: scenario

Minutes: 6

A student named Mara draws a dragon fight for art club and posts it on a school app. Minutes later, an AI moderation tool removes it. The notice says only: "Removed for violent content." It doesn't say which part was the problem, and it doesn't say who to ask. The app is made up.

[SCENARIO] What would you do?

- A. Accept it and move on. Feedback: You avoid a fight, but no one learns the tool made a mistake. Accountability only works if someone raises the problem.

- B. Appeal the decision. Feedback: A strong choice. An appeal asks someone to answer for the tool's call. Ask which part was flagged. If the app can't say, transparency is the problem.

- C. Post it again on another account. Feedback: It might work, but the tool could remove it again, and it could break the app's rules. It also fixes nothing for other students.

- D. Complain about the app in public. Feedback: Public pressure can get attention. It works better after an appeal fails, because then the company has had a chance to answer.

Follow-up: Who is responsible for the tool's mistake here?

Sources:

- None. Mara, the drawing, and the app are made up.

STEP 4: Quick check

Type: check

Minutes: 4

[CHECK] Someone is answerable when things go wrong. Which idea is that?

A. Fairness

B. Accountability

C. Transparency

D. Ethics

Answer: B. Accountability means someone is answerable when things go wrong.

[CHECK] A company won't say how its AI picks prize winners. Which idea is missing?

A. Fairness

B. Accountability

C. Transparency

D. A trade-off

Answer: C. Transparency means people can find out how a decision was made.

[CHECK] A school uses AI to pick students for a trip. Which question asks who is responsible?

A. Who is answerable if the tool picks the wrong students?

B. Which students are affected?

C. What could go wrong?

D. What would make it fairer?

Answer: A. The "who is responsible" question asks who answers for the tool's choices.

[CHECK] An app uses face scans to protect accounts. It is more secure, but it stores your face. What is the trade-off?

A. It costs more

B. It is slower

C. It has no downside

D. You get more security and give up some privacy

Answer: D. A trade-off means getting more of one thing by giving up some of another.

[CHECK] Your group must argue against AI cameras in parks. Which is the strongest way to defend that side?

A. "Cameras are bad."

B. "Everyone knows cameras are bad."

C. "Cameras can track people who did nothing wrong, so they should be limited."

D. "I don't care about parks."

Answer: C. A strong argument gives a reason, not just an opinion.

STEP 5: Cameras in public

Type: reflect

Minutes: 5

[REFLECT] Picture AI cameras in public places that look for problems, like a fight or a lost child. Where do you stand? What would change your mind? Write your answer on paper. This stays with you. Nobody else will see it.

[DISCUSS] Use the four questions to build your case. Your facilitator will tell you which side your group argues.

- Round 1: A city puts AI cameras in public parks to spot emergencies. Should it? Group 1 argues yes. Group 2 argues no.

- Round 2: Stores use AI cameras to catch shoplifters. Is it worth the loss of privacy? Group 1 argues no. Group 2 argues yes.

Before you start

- There is no single right answer in these cases. Assign sides for the debate so no one has to defend a personal view in front of the class.

- Reflect is private. Do not ask learners to share what they wrote.

- Every case is made up. Do not name a real company or product as the bad example.

- The camera topics can touch home or neighborhood life. Keep talk on the made up cases, not on learners' families.

- Split the class into Group 1 and Group 2 before Step 5. Give each group two minutes to prepare, then one minute per side.

Discussion prompts

- Which of the three ideas matters most for a hiring tool? Why?

- What should the removal notice in Step 3 have said?

- Did anyone change their mind during the debate? What changed it?

- Who pays the price when a tool makes a mistake?

Answer notes

- Step 1: no right answer. Look for learners naming a gain and something given up.

- Step 2 worked example: accept other reasonable answers if they name a person and a reason. The tool puts fairness at risk most. Transparency is at risk if applicants are not told.

- Step 3: B is the strongest because it asks someone to answer and asks what was flagged. Accept any choice a learner can explain with accountability or transparency.

- Step 4: B, C, A, D, C.

- Pre/post: C, A, B.

- Debate: judge the reasons, not the side. Praise a clear reason with a "because" in it.

## Depth pass: suggested upgrades (October 14, 2026)

From a review of all three courses. Nothing above this heading was changed. Rishi decides what to keep, then deletes this section. Claude opened every source below on October 14, 2026.

**Overall:** The four questions are a solid framework, and assigning debate sides is smart. The weakness is that every case is made up. You offered to use a real one. Here are two from schools, which middle schoolers will care about most.

### 1. Big idea for the header

**Big idea:** Most AI ethics questions aren't good versus bad. They are trade-offs, and the hard part is deciding who gets a say.

### 2. A real case for Step 1 or the debate (paste-ready)

[CALLOUT fact] In October 2025, a Baltimore County high school used an AI gun-detection system. It flagged a student's bag of chips as a weapon. Police handcuffed him before finding the chips. "I was just holding a Doritos bag," he said.

[CALLOUT think] The school wanted safety. The student paid for a false alarm. Who should check before anyone acts?

Source: WBAL-TV (October 2025) ->[https://www.wbaltv.com/article/student-handcuffed-ai-system-mistook-bag-chips-weapon/69114601](https://www.wbaltv.com/article/student-handcuffed-ai-system-mistook-bag-chips-weapon/69114601)

### 3. A second real case, about monitoring (Step 2)

[CALLOUT fact] A school district in Lawrence, Kansas, used an AI tool that scans students' school accounts. It sent over 1,200 alerts in about 10 months. Nearly two-thirds turned out to be nothing, including more than 200 caused by homework.

Source: AP, via Fortune (August 7, 2025) ->[https://www.fortune.com/2025/08/07/schools-ai-surveillance-students-children-arrested-jokes](https://www.fortune.com/2025/08/07/schools-ai-surveillance-students-children-arrested-jokes)

Facilitator note: this story shows false alarms. It doesn't show that one group was flagged more. Don't claim that.

### 4. A harder check question

[CHECK] A school's AI tool sends 100 alerts. Later, 65 turn out to be false alarms. What is the best next step?

A. Turn the tool off forever

B. Keep it, but have a person check every alert before anyone is punished

C. Punish all 100 students to be safe

D. Ignore all the alerts

*Answer: B. A person reviewing alerts keeps the safety benefit and catches the false alarms.*

### 5. ARK's view (new in guide 1.4)

Suggested for Step 5, before the reflect prompt:

**ARK's view:** We think an AI that can get a student in trouble needs a person to check first. A false alarm costs a real student.

**The best case against:** In a real emergency, waiting for a person can cost seconds that matter.

[REFLECT] Do you agree with ARK? What would change your mind?

### 6. Fixes

- The fairness definition, "treating people in an equal and reasonable way," is thin for grade 8. AI Fluent Module 3 shows that two fair-sounding rules can conflict. Consider: "Fairness: treating people in a way they could accept as just. People disagree about what that means."
